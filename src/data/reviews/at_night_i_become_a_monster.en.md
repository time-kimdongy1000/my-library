## 📖 Before reading

I have long been a fan of Yoru Sumino. Not that I know the author personally, of course. But after reading *I Want to Eat Your Pancreas* and being deeply shaken by it, I began seeking out more of Sumino's books.

Quite a few of them now sit on my bookshelf.

There is *I Want to Eat Your Pancreas*, the best-known of the bunch, along with *I Had That Same Dream Again*, *I Have a Secret*, and the subject of this review, *At Night, I Become a Monster*.

As its title suggests, *At Night, I Become a Monster* is a story about a monster. An actual monster appears in it. But that does not mean we should think of it as simply a fantasy novel.

The monster in this story is more than something that appears at night.

This is a novel about the monsters that might also exist within us.

So I want to talk about what I felt on rereading this book, and who the real monster in it might be.

---

## ✍️ After reading

### Who is the real monster?

There are, in fact, many monsters in this book. Right from the opening, the protagonist, Adachi, becomes one. Every night, countless black particles cover his body and transform him into a monster.

One night, already transformed, Adachi realizes that he has left his math homework at school and goes back to retrieve it. Just as he is about to leave with the worksheet, still in his monstrous form, he hears a girl's voice behind him.

#### You're Adachi, aren't you?

The girl's name is Yano. She is being bullied by her classmates.

It was not always that way. Because she spoke slowly and behaved a little differently from the others, a few students would quietly pick on her. But a single incident turns her into the most hated student in the class.

That incident involves a student named Midorikawa, who is quiet and has trouble fitting in with the others. One rainy day, Yano throws Midorikawa's book and pencil case out of the window for no apparent reason.

After that, the whole class turns against Yano, and the bullying begins in earnest.

The students who mistreat her fall broadly into three groups.

The first actively tease and bully her.

The second do not usually go out of their way to bully her, but pick on her whenever she enters their space.

The last do not actively join in, but go along with the general attitude that Yano is someone to be ignored.

Adachi belongs to that last group: the ones who do not get directly involved.

Now, back to that night at school, when I have come to collect my math homework. Yano immediately recognizes me, even though I have become a monster. She threatens to tell the others about my transformation unless I come to spend time with her at school at night.

Adachi does not want anyone to find out that he becomes a monster, so he has little choice but to agree. In the daytime, he would simply have brushed Yano off. But he cannot do that now.

After talking to her for a little while, he learns that she has just one reason for coming to school at night.

She cannot get a break during the breaks at school in the daytime.

Adachi does not quite understand what she means, but lets it pass. They part after agreeing to meet again the following night.

The next morning.

Yano greets everyone in her usual odd way of speaking, but no one answers.

As soon as she sits down, she jumps up in surprise. Someone has poured water onto her chair before she arrived.

She has no choice but to go and get a cloth.

But no one pays any attention to what has happened to her.

They act as though there is no one named Yano in the classroom at all.

They can clearly hear her voice. They simply pretend they cannot.

They keep treating her as if she does not exist.

Night falls again.

Having turned into a monster, I reluctantly make my way to school. Yano is there too.

She asks me a question.

***Which is the real you: Adachi by day, or Adachi by night?***

I do not understand the question.

Surely this monstrous form cannot be the real me. Yet here she is, asking me something so strange.

I do not answer.

I am the same person, whether it is day or night.

And once again, we talk.

It would be unthinkable in the daytime classroom. Yet as a monster at night, I find myself drawn into what she has to say, and the conversation continues.

Though to call it a conversation might be generous: mostly, Yano fires questions at me. Occasionally, when I ask something, she answers. But whenever it concerns the daytime, she holds to the same strange rule: do not ask about school during the day.

And I find myself thinking:

Maybe I can see, a little, why this girl gets bullied.

And so the bullied girl and I, the monster, keep meeting in a classroom at night, when neither of us should be there.

That is the opening part of the story.

## ⭐ What stayed with me

### Which is the real you: Adachi by day, or Adachi by night?

I think this question runs through the entire book. Again and again, the story explores what is real and what is false, and the relationship between what people show on the outside and what they carry within.

One scene in particular stayed with me. Adachi has a friend named Kudo. She belongs to the group that actively picks on Yano whenever Yano enters their space.

One day, while walking with Adachi, Kudo spots Yano. She throws the plastic cup she has been using, hitting Yano squarely in the back of the head.

But what struck me even more was what happened next.

Kudo simply carries on talking to Adachi as though nothing has happened.

Watching her, Adachi wonders:

Which is the real Kudo?

The Kudo who threw her used plastic cup at Yano?  
Or the Kudo who is now chatting with me as if everything is perfectly normal?

He finds himself wondering which of them is real.

But Kudo is one person. She does not have separate personalities. The person who threw the cup at Yano is Kudo, and the person talking to Adachi is Kudo too.

Through her, I think Sumino is showing us that a human being cannot be reduced to a single face. Someone can be a treasured friend to one person and the cruelest person in the world to another.

How, then, can we judge a person?

### So which self is real?

The answer to Yano's question is that both are the same person.

But it seems to me that we go through life wearing whichever mask serves us best in our current circumstances and surroundings.

For Adachi by day, the mask of someone who ignores Yano makes it easier to survive. The moment he gets close to her, he risks being rejected by the group himself.

At night, however, Yano is the only one who knows he is a monster, and he has no need to put on a mask. Adachi is actually much more honest at night than he is during the day.

And as the story goes on, his nighttime self grows closer and closer to Yano.

Ultimately, Adachi by day and Adachi by night are not different people.

They are both the same person: Adachi.

He simply shows different sides of himself depending on the situation he is in.

### Adachi is the real monster

Adachi eventually comes to realize that he himself is the real monster.

At first, ignoring Yano is as natural to him as breathing. He does not return her greetings, and he looks the other way when she is bullied. He does not even give much thought to whether it is wrong.

But as he keeps talking with her at school at night, he slowly begins to change.

He comes to understand why she had to act as she did, and sees a side of her completely different from the one he thought he knew. He also discovers that she is far kinder than he had imagined.

The closer he gets to Yano, the harder it becomes for him to be at school during the day.

What once came as naturally as breathing now takes a conscious effort.

He has to ignore Yano.

But now he knows.

He knows what kind of person he is ignoring.

And so he becomes like someone swimming in the middle of the sea, with no ground beneath his feet. He keeps moving, trying to get somewhere, but he has already swallowed so much seawater that he has no idea how much longer he can hold on.

Then Adachi begins to question himself.

Why am I staying with the people who bully Yano?

At least they know which side they are on. They have chosen to stand with the bullies, and they act on that choice.

But I have not.

I do not bully Yano myself.

But I do not help her either.

At night I talk to her and grow closer to her, only to pretend I do not know her once the day comes.

I feel sorry for her, but I am afraid of becoming a target myself.

I want to be close to her, but I am afraid of losing my place in the group.

In the end, I have never truly taken either side. I have only chosen whichever position keeps me safest.

That is why Adachi comes to think that he, of all people, is the real monster.

To me, this is the part that best expresses what the novel means by a monster.

A monster is not only someone who directly attacks another person.

Sometimes it can be someone who knows something is wrong, yet looks away to protect themselves.

And that person was Adachi himself.

## 💭 My own thoughts

I think we may be rather like chameleons. We change ourselves into whatever form gives us the best chance in the environment we find ourselves in. Perhaps that is what our masks are.

Perhaps the only place where we can take off our masks is somewhere we are completely alone. Everywhere else, we wear a mask to some extent in order to live among other people.

But I do not want to criticize the mere act of wearing one. I think some degree of masking is unavoidable when we live in a society.

Can we really go through life showing our whole, unguarded selves to everyone we meet?

I find myself wondering how many people could actually live that way.

That may be why I think Adachi is brave when, at the end, he takes off his mask within the small society of the classroom.

The moment he returns Yano's greeting, he stops hiding which side he is on.

He must have known that the others would look at him differently because of that choice. Even so, this time he does not put his mask back on to keep himself safe.

I think this is the first real choice Adachi makes in the book.

---

## 🌙 Closing thoughts

Through the subject of bullying, Yoru Sumino writes about human nature and where our true selves lie.

Perhaps being a monster has less to do with the shape we take on the outside than with the cruelty we carry within.
