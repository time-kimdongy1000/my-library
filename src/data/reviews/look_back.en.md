## 📖 Before Reading

One day, a video appeared in my YouTube feed in which a Seoul National University professor discussed and interpreted *Look Back*. As I watched it, I remembered hearing a few months earlier that *Look Back* was being adapted into a live-action film. I looked it up, learned that it would open nationwide on October 8, 2026, and booked a ticket for opening day.

I have now experienced the original manga, the animated adaptation, and the live-action film. Each one felt as though it were telling my own story. Today, I want to write about *Look Back*.

## ✍️ After Reading

### Turning inferiority into something beautiful

An inferiority complex is usually understood as something negative. Yet Tatsuki Fujimoto uses two girls to turn that feeling into something beautiful.

To Fujino, Kyomoto was the source of her insecurity. After meeting, however, the two girls begin to look to each other's backs, lean on each other, and move forward. That is why I think this work transforms inferiority into a beautiful story.

### What the back means

As the title *Look Back* suggests, the image of a person's back plays an important role in this work. I want to discuss three such images that stood out to me. The two girls grow by watching each other's backs.

### The first back

This is a coming-of-age story about two girls. The first back appears when Fujino begins looking toward Kyomoto.

Fujino had always dominated the four-panel manga section of the school newspaper. Then Kyomoto, a girl with extraordinary talent, suddenly appears. The quality of Kyomoto's art is so overwhelming that Fujino can hardly compare herself to it. Fujino immediately runs to a bookstore, buys books, and throws herself into studying art. No matter how hard she works, however, she cannot catch up with Kyomoto's back. Eventually, she stops drawing.

Then, on graduation day, Fujino goes to deliver Kyomoto's diploma and finally meets her. As Fujino turns to leave almost as if she were running away, Kyomoto says:

“Fujino-sensei, I'm a fan of your manga. Please give me your autograph.”

Fujino is stunned. The brilliant girl whose overwhelming talent crushed her confidence and drove her to give up drawing has just said something completely unexpected. “Fujino-sensei.” Those words lead Fujino back to the art she had abandoned.

I felt that the image of Fujino running through the rain was beautifully rendered in both the animated and live-action adaptations. It is difficult to put into words, but in that scene I saw what can happen when someone who has surrendered in the face of talent is suddenly met with admiration and praise.

### The second back

In the second image, Kyomoto is the one looking at Fujino's back.

Afraid of other people, Kyomoto has lived shut away in her room. Fujino, however, draws her outside. By making art with Fujino, Kyomoto connects with the world beyond her room for the first time.

She is still afraid of people, but Fujino is always there. In the manga, the animated adaptation, and the live-action film, the scene of Fujino walking ahead through town while Kyomoto holds on behind her is beautiful. On the train home, Kyomoto thanks Fujino for bringing her out of her room.

### The third back

The final back is the one Fujino faces after Kyomoto is gone.

After Kyomoto dies in a tragic incident, Fujino attends her funeral and visits her home and studio. Kyomoto is no longer in this world, yet her work still covers the room as if she might return and resume drawing at any moment. On the door hangs the same piece of clothing Fujino signed when they were in sixth grade.

At that moment, a question returns: “Why does Fujino draw?” Fujino then races back to her studio and begins drawing again.

As a child, Fujino heard that Kyomoto admired her, ran home through the rain, and returned to drawing. As an adult, after losing Kyomoto, she once again runs to her studio and begins to draw.

The two scenes resemble each other, yet they felt entirely different to me. I understood the first run. The girl Fujino thought was a genius had admired her all along. But I honestly do not understand the second one. Why did Fujino begin drawing again?

Perhaps only Fujino knows the answer. All I could see until the very end was Fujino's back as she sat at her desk and drew once more. That is the third back I found in the story.

## 🌙 Closing Thoughts

Perhaps the time I spent with the manga, the animated adaptation, and the live-action film was also a chance to look back at myself. The first thing I felt after watching the film was regret. Why had I never been as sincere about the things I love as the girls in the story? Why had I not started doing what I loved sooner?

Yet as I returned to the manga, the animation, and the film, I began to feel something else. Even as a fully grown adult, I could still see Fujino's back ahead of me.

I do not have an extraordinary talent like Fujino or Kyomoto. I am simply an ordinary adult who goes to work every day. But I think I now understand at least a little of the joy of making something.

I run a small YouTube channel. I post short videos every day and longer videos once a week. Doing this while working full-time means constantly searching for ideas and researching material, then writing scripts, producing and editing videos, and adding subtitles—all by myself. It is always difficult, and sometimes it is not enjoyable at all.

Nor do viewers suddenly become enthusiastic when I publish the finished work. My channel is very small, and I receive almost no reward for uploading videos.

Even so, when I finish a video, the fear of being judged is joined by the joy of having made one more work and sent it out into the world. I feel that I may be able to answer the question Fujino could not answer so easily.

The process of devoting myself to YouTube and making something that earns no money can be painful, exhausting, and sometimes dull. Still, the moment a finished work enters the world is intensely joyful and exhilarating. Perhaps it is the taste of that moment that brings me back to my desk to edit again today.
